# GUMFALL — Intro Cinematic

**Cross-references:** [Premise and Tone](PREMISE_AND_TONE.md) · [Character Creation](../game-design/CHARACTER_CREATION.md) · [Body Integrity](../systems/BODY_INTEGRITY.md) · [GelFlow Combat](../systems/GELFLOW_COMBAT.md)

## 1. Overview and Design Intent

The GUMFALL intro cinematic serves six simultaneous purposes:

1. **Scale establishment** — Show the player the full scope of the world before shrinking to human scale.
2. **Tonal contract** — Establish all four tonal pillars (epic, tragic, comedic, heroic) before a word of gameplay dialogue is spoken.
3. **Mechanical priming** — Introduce limb separation as a real, normalized feature of the world before the tutorial asks the player to engage with it.
4. **Tutorial companion introduction** — Bring in the one-armed Bearkin soldier as the tutorial voice in a way that is earned, not arbitrary.
5. **Title delivery** — The word GUMFALL must land with weight. The cinematic earns the title before displaying it.
6. **Player invitation** — The cinematic ends with an address to the player ("everyone") that signals they are now part of the world, not watching it.

The cinematic is approximately 90–120 seconds (PROPOSED: exact timing TBD by implementation). It should be skippable after a player has completed it at least once, but the skip option should require deliberate input (not accidental).

## 2. Full Sequence — Beat by Beat

### Beat 1: Total Darkness (Duration: PROPOSED ~4–6 seconds)

**Visual:** Complete black. No UI. No music yet. Silence.

**Design Note:** Do not rush this. The darkness is not loading — it is intentional negative space. The player should wonder, briefly, if something has gone wrong. This uncertainty primes the scale reveal.

**Audio:** Silence. Possibly the faintest trace of a low crystalline hum, below conscious perception — the Sugar Lattice, before the sequence names it.

---

### Beat 2: Sky (Duration: PROPOSED ~3–4 seconds)

**Visual:** Cut from black to open sky. The sky is candy-colored — not cartoony but genuinely beautiful. Pastels at the edge of the horizon, deeper rich hues overhead. The sky itself is slightly translucent, as if made of stained hard candy rather than air. No ground visible yet.

**Audio:** Music begins — low strings, building. Something that sounds ancient and large.

**Director Note:** The sky should feel physically real. This is not a cartoon sky. The clouds, if present, should have texture — spun sugar, maybe. But the overwhelming impression is *size*.

---

### Beat 3: Cosmic Scale — Pull Back (Duration: PROPOSED ~8–10 seconds)

**Visual:** The camera pulls back slowly. The sky becomes a planet. The planet is the Sweetlands — visible from orbit, a sphere of candy-geography. It is beautiful and detailed. Crystalline mountain ranges catch light. Syrup seas shimmer. There are visible fracture lines across its surface (the Shattering has already happened — this is a post-Shattering world).

**Audio:** Music swells. The scale should feel earned, not imposed. This is the cosmological context of the game world.

**Director Note:** The fracture lines visible from orbit are not highlighted or labeled. They are just there. The player may not consciously register them as significant yet — they will understand their significance retroactively when the narrative explains the Shattering.

**Accessibility Note:** This sequence involves slow camera movement through large space. Motion sensitivity options (see Section 9) should be configured before the game's first launch. An option to display the cinematic at a fixed camera angle rather than a pull-back should be available.

---

### Beat 4: Scale Down — Continent (Duration: PROPOSED ~4–5 seconds)

**Visual:** The camera descends rapidly toward the planet's surface. The Sweetlands continent comes into focus. The fracture pattern of the Shattering is now clearly visible — the continent is broken into floating fragments at various altitudes. The overall impression is of a shattered plate: pieces present, but no longer connected.

**Audio:** Music pulls back slightly. The silence at the edges emphasizes the gaps between fragments.

**Director Note:** This is the first moment the Shattering is shown as an environmental reality, not a narrative fact. Players should feel it physically. The gaps between fragments should look like they hurt.

---

### Beat 5: Scale Down — Battlefield (Duration: PROPOSED ~6–8 seconds)

**Visual:** The camera continues descending, homing in on a specific fragment. A battlefield comes into focus — chaotic, active. Gummy armies of different lineages are in conflict. The combat is physical and real: bodies stretch, compress, and occasionally lose parts. Limbs scatter. Fighters reattach what they can. This is not graphic horror — it is the physical reality of gummy warfare, at once brutal and occasionally absurd.

**Audio:** The battlefield noise builds. The music is now overlaid with the sounds of conflict — not gore sounds, but the sounds of a gummy world in combat. The occasional elastic *thwack* and crystalline *crack* of bodies impacting.

**Director Note:** Show at least one clear example of limb separation in this sequence — a soldier losing an arm, a fighter losing a leg and continuing to fight. This normalizes the mechanic before the tutorial. It should look like it hurts but also like it is survivable. Both things simultaneously.

---

### Beat 6: Scale Down — Giant Red Gummy Bear (Duration: PROPOSED ~4–6 seconds)

**Visual:** The camera descends further and *stops* on a single figure: a massive red gummy bear. The bear is not a player character — this is a creature, huge, ancient-looking, watching the conflict from above with something like sorrow. It does not intervene. Its eyes track the battlefield below.

**Audio:** The battlefield sounds fade. Music shifts — something older, slower. The giant bear's breath is audible.

**Director Note:** The giant red gummy bear is PROPOSED as a cosmological or mythological figure — possibly a god-remnant of the Lattice, possibly a memory-echo, possibly something else (UNKNOWN, requires narrative decision). At this stage, it is simply a presence: large, red, watching, sad. Do not explain it. Do not label it. Let it breathe.

**Status Note:** The exact nature and narrative function of the giant red gummy bear is UNKNOWN. This is a deliberate narrative mystery. Writers should not retroactively assign it a role without owner approval.

---

### Beat 7: Gummy Armies in Conflict (Duration: PROPOSED ~5–7 seconds)

**Visual:** Cut back to the battlefield — now at eye level within the conflict. The perspective moves through the battle like a witness: we see different lineages fighting, different weapons, different body types. Armor takes damage. Weapons stick into bodies and are pulled free. The chaos is organized enough to be readable. Factions are distinguishable by color and equipment (not labeled yet).

**Audio:** Full conflict soundscape. Music underneath.

**Director Note:** This is the moment to show the breadth of the game's physical systems in silhouette: parries, limb locks, armor deflections. A viewer should be able to see the *systems* at work without understanding them yet.

---

### Beat 8: GUMFALL Title (Duration: PROPOSED ~3–4 seconds)

**Visual:** From the heart of the conflict, a moment of stillness — the camera catches a gap between fighters. In that gap, the battlefield is briefly quiet. Then:

**GUMFALL**

The title appears. Not animated with flash or particle effects — it *lands*. Large. Simple. The letters should feel heavy. The font (PROPOSED: decided in visual identity work) should carry weight without being ornate.

**Audio:** Music resolves to a single note — held. The title appears on the held note.

**Director Note:** The title must be earned. If the preceding beats have done their work, the title should feel inevitable and correct. If we need to dress it up with effects, the preceding beats have failed.

---

### Beat 9: The Soldier (Duration: PROPOSED ~5–8 seconds)

**Visual:** Fade from the title back into the battlefield — but now the camera is focused on a single figure. A red Bearkin soldier. Medium height, battered armor (bronze, consistent with the vertical slice), clearly exhausted. The soldier is missing one arm. Not recently — the wound is old. The stump is clean-edged, like a separation that happened some time ago and has not been reattached.

The soldier is not fighting at this moment. They are looking at the player — at the camera. As if they have been waiting.

**Audio:** Battlefield sounds recede. Personal scale. The soldier's breath. A pause.

**Director Note:** The missing arm must be legible on first view. Deliberately prominent without being the only thing visible. This is not a tragedy to flinch from — it is a fact about this character, worn openly.

---

### Beat 10: The Line (Duration: PROPOSED ~3–4 seconds)

The red Bearkin soldier speaks the first words of the game:

> **"Before we go anywhere, I would like everyone to count their arms."**

**Performance Note (PROPOSED):** The line is delivered as if it is a reasonable and normal thing to say. Not comedic emphasis. Not dramatic weight. A practical suggestion from someone who has seen enough to know it matters. The warmth is understated. The humor emerges from the contrast between the world-scale of the preceding cinematic and the physical specificity of this request — not from the delivery itself.

**VERIFIED:** This scripted line is locked. Do not alter the text.

---

### Beat 11: Transition to Tutorial (Duration: PROPOSED ~2–3 seconds)

**Visual:** The soldier either gestures for the player to follow, or the scene cuts to the tutorial environment — a compact arena (see Vertical Slice documentation). The transition should feel continuous with the cinematic, not like a cut to a menu or loading screen.

**Audio:** Music transitions to the tutorial ambient track.

## 3. The Tutorial Companion — The One-Armed Soldier

### Identity

**Status: PROPOSED — final character design requires owner approval**

The one-armed red Bearkin soldier is PROPOSED as the primary tutorial companion. Key proposed attributes:

- **Lineage:** Bearkin (PROPOSED)
- **Composition:** PROPOSED — likely Firm-Set or Soft-Set, reflecting their durability but also their visibility of damage
- **Background:** Broken Veteran (PROPOSED — thematically appropriate)
- **Class:** PROPOSED — likely Edgebearer (sword-arm missing, fighting adapted) or Gummy Brawler (physical combat style)
- **Missing arm:** Left arm (PROPOSED — right-arm combat primary). The missing arm is not a handicap — it is a completed adaptation. The soldier has fully restructured their fighting style around one arm.
- **Name:** UNKNOWN — requires narrative owner decision

### Tutorial Voice Role

The soldier's job is to teach the player GelFlow combat mechanics while inhabiting the world authentically. They do not break the fourth wall. They do not say "press this button." They describe what to do in world-language and the game translates that to control prompts.

Example tutorial voice (PROPOSED draft):

> *"Your body knows what to do. Let it. When something comes at you, your instinct is to be somewhere else — lean into that."* [Maps to dodge tutorial]

> *"Armor stops shape. If you want to move something, think about where its armor isn't."* [Maps to body-region targeting tutorial]

> *"Reattachment takes proximity and a moment of stillness. I've gotten very good at finding moments of stillness."* [Maps to limb recovery tutorial — and lands differently given their missing arm]

### The Missing Arm in Tutorial Context

The missing arm serves a tutorial function: it normalizes limb separation before the player experiences it themselves. The soldier talks about limb loss the way someone talks about a familiar inconvenience. The player sees the consequences (changed fighting style, visible absence) without seeing the soldier distressed about it.

When the tutorial covers limb recovery mechanics, the soldier's missing arm becomes the clearest possible teaching moment:

> *"Mine isn't coming back. Situation was what it was. But yours will. That's what Sugar Wells are for."*

This line accomplishes several things simultaneously:
1. Validates the mechanic (limb recovery exists and is normal)
2. Acknowledges that permanent loss is also possible (raises stakes)
3. Tells us something true about the soldier's history
4. Keeps the tone balanced between tragedy and practicality

## 4. Gameplay Integration — The Arm Count Tutorial

### "Count Your Arms" as Mechanical Introduction

The first line — "count their arms" — is the transition point from cinematic to gameplay. The tutorial is structured around this framing:

**Step 1 — Count Your Arms:** The game asks the player, after the cinematic, to open the body integrity interface. The first time it opens, a simplified overlay highlights the player character's body regions. The soldier narrates: *"Good. You've got what you came in with. Let's keep it that way."*

**Step 2 — Body Region Familiarity:** The tutorial introduces the concept of per-region integrity through guided practice, not lecture.

**Step 3 — First Hit:** The player takes a scripted hit in the tutorial that causes visible body deformation to one region. The soldier reacts practically: *"That's going to bruise. Keep moving — standing still is how you lose more than an arm."*

**Step 4 — Sugar Well Introduction:** The tutorial culminates in reaching a Sugar Well, which restores the damaged region. The soldier's line about their own missing arm (see above) plays here.

### Tutorial Scope

The intro cinematic tutorial covers (VERIFIED per Vertical Slice documentation):
- Body integrity interface navigation
- Basic movement and dodge
- Light attack and heavy attack
- Parry introduction (not mastery)
- Body-region targeting (introduction)
- Limb separation and recovery
- Sugar Well usage

The tutorial does **not** cover:
- Class-specific abilities (introduced at Level 1 selection)
- Weapon mastery
- Crafting or economy
- Companion management
- Advanced combat mechanics

## 5. Director Notes — Full Cinematic

### Pacing

The cinematic moves from macro to micro: universe → planet → continent → battlefield → giant → battle → title → soldier → line. Each scale change should feel like a breath, not a jump cut. The total duration (PROPOSED: 90–120 seconds excluding title card hold) is long for a modern intro, which is deliberate. The scale must be earned.

### Color Language

- **Red:** The player-adjacent color. The Bearkin soldier is red. This is intentional color branding for the tutorial companion. Red = presence, warmth, life.
- **Fracture lines:** Cold blue-white where the Sugar Lattice is broken. This is the color of absence.
- **Gold/amber:** The Sugar Lattice intact. Used sparingly — now mostly gone.
- **Dark colors:** Enemy factions, ambiguous moral actors. No explicit color-coding, but darkness leans toward opposition.

### Audio Design

| Beat | Music Approach | SFX |
|------|---------------|-----|
| 1 — Darkness | Silence / subliminal hum | None |
| 2 — Sky | Low strings begin | Wind |
| 3 — Cosmic pull-back | Full orchestral swell | None |
| 4 — Continent | Music pulls back | None |
| 5 — Battlefield | Music over conflict sounds | Combat SFX |
| 6 — Giant Bear | Music slows, ancient texture | Bear breath |
| 7 — Armies | Full SFX mix | All conflict |
| 8 — Title | Single held note | None |
| 9 — Soldier | Personal scale, minimal music | Breath, armor |
| 10 — The Line | Music holds | Voice only |
| 11 — Transition | Tutorial ambient begins | Fade |

### Visual Style Consistency

The cinematic should be rendered in the same visual style as gameplay — not a pre-rendered video that diverges from the game's look. (PROPOSED: confirmation required based on engine selection. See [Architecture](../technical/ARCHITECTURE.md).) If pre-rendered cutscenes are used for performance reasons, the cinematic must maintain visual consistency with the gameplay aesthetic.

## 6. What the Cinematic Establishes

### Mechanically Established

| Mechanic | How Established |
|----------|----------------|
| Limb separation is normal | Shown in battle sequence; confirmed by soldier's missing arm |
| Bodies deform and reattach | Shown in battle sequence |
| Body regions are tracked | Not shown directly; tutorial extends from cinematic |
| Sugar Wells restore integrity | Referenced in tutorial extension |
| GelFlow physics | Demonstrated in battle sequence silhouette |

### Tonally Established

| Tone Pillar | How Established |
|-------------|----------------|
| Epic Fantasy | Cosmic scale, armies, world scope |
| Tragedy | Fracture lines, displacement, the gap between fragments |
| Physical Comedy | Limb loss treated as normal, "count your arms" |
| Sincere Heroism | The soldier — battered, missing an arm, still here, still talking to you |

### Narratively Established

| Narrative Element | How Established |
|-------------------|----------------|
| The Shattering has already happened | Fracture lines visible from orbit |
| The world is broken but alive | People still fighting |
| A mystery exists (the Giant Bear) | Shown, not explained |
| The player has a guide | Soldier addresses "everyone" directly |
| This is going to be hard | Battlefield scale, genuine danger visible |

## 7. Scripted Line — Protection and Preservation

The following line is **locked** and must not be altered in implementation:

> **"Before we go anywhere, I would like everyone to count their arms."**

This applies to:
- In-game audio
- Subtitle text
- Any marketing materials quoting the line
- Localization source text (translations may adapt for natural language, but the meaning must be preserved exactly)

**Localization Note:** The joke depends on the assumption that arm-counting is a reasonable safety check — which it is, in this world. Translations must preserve the mundane practicality of the request, not heighten or diminish it. If a language cannot make arm-counting feel practical, the localization team should flag it for creative review.

## 8. Alternate Viewing Modes

The cinematic will be available in the following viewing modes after first playthrough:

1. **Full cinematic** — Default, full sequence.
2. **Skip** — Bypass to character creation or last checkpoint after first completion.
3. **Replay** — Available from main menu or options.
4. **Accessibility replay** — Static-camera version available from accessibility settings (see Section 9).

## 9. Accessibility Notes

### Motion Sensitivity

The cosmic pull-back sequence (Beats 2–4) involves significant camera movement through large space and rapid scale changes. The following options should be available before the cinematic plays:

- **Standard mode** — Full cinematic as designed.
- **Reduced motion mode** — Camera movements slowed by 60% (PROPOSED: exact value TBD by implementation). Scale changes use dissolves rather than continuous movement.
- **Static camera mode** — Each beat is presented as a still frame with narration, no camera movement.

### Subtitles

- All spoken dialogue must have subtitle support, including the first line spoken by the tutorial companion.
- Subtitles must be visible before the player has had a chance to configure accessibility settings — default subtitle behavior should be ON or should be configurable at first launch.
- Speaker identification should be visible in subtitles (e.g., "[Soldier]" before the line).
- Subtitle size, contrast, and background opacity must be configurable.

### Screen Brightness and Flash

- No flashing cuts in the cinematic. Transitions are fades or dissolves.
- The transition from complete darkness (Beat 1) to bright sky (Beat 2) should be gradual (PROPOSED: 0.5–1.0 second fade-in).

### Audio Description

- An audio description track (PROPOSED: optional, additional production) should describe on-screen events for players with visual impairments. This track would narrate: the scale changes, the armies, the Giant Bear, the soldier's appearance and missing arm.
- The audio description track must not overlap the soldier's spoken line.

### Timing and Pacing

- The cinematic should not require any player input until after the soldier has spoken the first line. No QTEs, no button prompts during the cinematic itself.
- For players with cognitive processing preferences, the ability to pause and resume the cinematic should be available after first line delivery.
